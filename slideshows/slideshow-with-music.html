The short answer

A slideshow maker with music suits photos that can carry the message alone. Most business slideshows need more: a voiceover that says what the photos can’t, such as the price, size, location or next step, with music as a quiet bed underneath. Use only music your licence covers wherever you publish, keep it well below the voice and caption everything. Videngine makes narrated slideshows with an optional music bed under the voice.

Music only or music under a voiceover

Music alone works when the photos explain themselves; when the viewer needs facts, the music should sit under a voiceover that states them.

Any photo video maker with music will set 30 photos to a track. Music alone suits a mood piece: a hotel at dusk, an event recap. It suits a listing badly, because a photo of a kitchen cannot tell a buyer the asking price or the number of bedrooms.

For most business slideshows the music is a bed: a low, steady track under narration that names what is on screen and adds what isn’t.

Music rights for business use

Use only music whose licence covers business use on every platform where the video will appear, and keep a record of it.

Assume a published video needs permission for both the recording and the song, and that the licence decides where you may publish. Read the terms before a track goes into a batch of 200 videos.

YouTube’s Content ID help says every upload is scanned against audio and visual files submitted by copyright owners; on a match, the owner’s settings decide whether the video is blocked, monetised or tracked. YouTube’s page on copyright claims says a claim is not a strike and usually doesn’t affect your channel; you may be able to swap the claimed audio for an Audio Library track, and should dispute only if you’re confident you have the rights.

YouTube calls its Audio Library, in YouTube Studio, copyright-safe: tracks downloaded from it won’t be claimed through Content ID, and Creative Commons tracks need the artist credited in the description. YouTube also says it can’t give legal guidance on music off the platform. If the video is going to your website or an ad account too, check the licence for those places.

Choose instrumental music, because lyrics compete with the narration, and a track that stays level throughout. A slideshow cut to a script won’t land on the track’s big moments: a chorus at 40 seconds arrives mid-sentence about parking.

How to mix music under a voice

Set the voice first, then bring the music up underneath until it is felt more than heard.

These are rules of thumb, not standards. Trust your ears on the device your viewers use.

  • Level: a common starting point is music 15 to 20 dB below the voice while it speaks. If you can follow the melody during a sentence, it is too loud.
  • Ducking: lower the music under speech, let it rise a little in pauses and fade it out at the end.
  • Arrangement: sparse tracks leave room for the voice; busy piano or strings in the speech range bury it.
  • Checks: listen on a phone speaker for clarity and on headphones for clutter.
  • Batches: keep one level and one family of tracks across a batch, so 50 listing videos sound like one business.

If a viewer would miss nothing with the music muted, the mix is about right. Our guide to AI voiceover covers choosing a voice and getting pronunciation right.

Captions for viewers with the sound off

Caption every narrated slideshow, because the voice only reaches viewers who have the sound on.

Plan for people watching in a feed, at work or on a train with the sound muted: for them, captions and on-screen text carry the whole message. Watch every video once with the sound off before it goes out.

Captions also matter for access. The W3C’s captions guidance says captions for prerecorded video are required at WCAG Level A, and serve people who cannot hear the audio and people who process written information better than audio.

A music-only slideshow has nothing to caption, so its facts must sit on screen as text. Our short-form video guide covers vertical cuts for Shorts, Reels and TikTok.

How to create a video with pictures and music

To create a video with pictures and music for a business, write the words first, fit the photos to them and lay the music under both.

  1. Decide the job. Music only, or music under narration.
  2. Choose and order the photos. Wide shots first, then details. Our guide on how to make a slideshow covers selection and order.
  3. Write the script. One idea per photo, facts the photos cannot show and a clear next step.
  4. Record the voiceover. Check that names, numbers and places are said correctly.
  5. Time the photos to the voice. Change photo on a sentence, not on a beat.
  6. Choose a licensed track. Confirm the licence covers every platform, and note it.
  7. Mix. Voice first, music underneath, ducked under speech and faded at both ends.
  8. Caption from the final audio. Burned in for feeds; a caption file for YouTube and your site.
  9. Export and check. A 9:16 and a 16:9 cut; watch each with the sound off, then on a phone speaker.

Questions about choosing a slideshow maker with music

Can I use any song in a slideshow with music for my business?

No. Use only music whose licence covers business use on every platform where the video will appear. Don’t assume a purchase or streaming subscription covers publishing, and read the terms of any “royalty-free” track.

Will YouTube claim a picture video with music?

It can. YouTube’s Content ID scans every upload against files submitted by copyright owners, and a match lets the owner block, monetise or track the video. YouTube says music downloaded from its Audio Library won’t be claimed through Content ID, and that a claim is not a copyright strike.

How loud should music be under a voiceover?

Quiet enough that every word is clear on a phone speaker. A common rule of thumb is music 15 to 20 dB below the voice while it speaks, rising a little in pauses. Judge the final mix by ear.

Should a photo video with music also have narration?

For a listing, product or explainer, usually yes. Photos cannot say the price, size, location or next step, and a voice can. Keep the music as a quiet bed underneath and caption the narration.
