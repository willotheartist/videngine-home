How to make a slideshow video: photos, order, timing and music

How to make a slideshow video for a business: how many photos, what resolution, story order, timing to narration, music, captions and 16:9 or 9:16 formats.

The short answer

How to make a slideshow video, in short: choose about 8 sharp photos for 30 seconds or 12 to 16 for a minute, put them in story order (establish, detail, close), give each photo one sentence of narration, add a few information cards, a slow zoom, quiet music under the voice and captions, then export 16:9 and 9:16 cuts. Videngine does most of this automatically from a listing, a product page or a row of data, using your own photos.

Choose the photos: how many, and how sharp

Use fewer photos than you have, and only ones sharp enough to fill the frame: as a rule of thumb, 7 to 9 for a 30-second video and 12 to 16 for 60 seconds.

That assumes three to four seconds of narration per photo, plus title and closing cards. A listing with 40 photos needs the few that answer the viewer’s questions.

A 1080p frame is 1,920 pixels on its long side, landscape or vertical, and a slideshow enlarges every photo before zooming into it. Videngine grades each photo by the long side of the original file:

  • Sharp: 1,600 px or more.
  • OK: from 1,000 px. Usable, a little soft once enlarged.
  • Low: below 1,000 px. The studio flags it.

Smaller photos are enlarged with a Lanczos filter and a light sharpen, never more than 3×, but enlarging cannot add detail the camera never caught. Websites often serve resized copies, so Videngine asks for the original first; do the same by hand. Drop near-duplicates, other sites’ watermarks and anything out of date.

Put the photos in story order

Order the photos as a short story: establish what and where, show the details that matter, then close on the best image and the next step.

A viewer cannot flick back in a video, so the order does the work: open wide, then move through the details as someone would in person, outside to inside, whole to part.

SubjectEstablishDetailClose
PropertyFront of the building or the streetLiving room, kitchen, bedrooms, garden, floor planThe best view, then a card on booking a viewing
CarThree-quarter front view, outdoorsSide profile, interior, dashboard, boot, service bookRear three-quarter view, then a card with the key specification
ProductThe product on its own, clearly litIn use, a materials close-up, scale, colour optionsThe product in context, then a card saying where to buy it

A venue (entrance, rooms, an event set-up) or a project (before, during, after) follows the same pattern. See real estate listing videos, video for car dealers and product video automation for each subject.

Time each photo to a sentence, then add cards

Let the narration set the timing: each photo stays on screen for the sentence that explains it, and no longer.

A fixed interval gives every photo the same weight; a sentence per photo gives the important ones more time and says why they matter. As a rule of thumb, a ten-word sentence takes about four seconds at a calm pace, so 30 seconds holds roughly 70 to 80 words.

Write about what the photo cannot show. “Here is the kitchen” wastes four seconds; “The kitchen was refitted last year and opens onto the garden” earns them. A floor plan, or any photo with text to read, needs longer.

Cards carry facts photos cannot: dimensions, mileage, opening hours, a specification. Use a title card for what and where, information cards for one fact each, held long enough to read twice, and a closing card for the next step. Keep card text inside the safe area, because vertical feeds lay buttons over the edges.

Add motion, music and captions

Give each photo a slow, steady zoom or pan, keep any music quiet under the voice, and caption every word.

The usual motion is the Ken Burns effect: a slow zoom or pan across the photo, ideally towards the subject. Fast zooms, spins and novelty transitions distract; a short fade between photos is enough.

Music is optional. If you use it, choose an instrumental track, keep it low enough that every word stays clear, and check that the licence covers commercial use. See slideshow with music and narration for choosing and mixing a track.

Captions serve sound-off viewers and make the video accessible: WCAG success criterion 1.2.2 asks for captions on all prerecorded audio in synchronised media, so that people who are deaf or hard of hearing can follow it. Make them from the final audio so the words on screen match the words spoken.

Export for each screen: 16:9, 9:16, 1:1 and 4:5

Make one cut for each place the video will run, usually a 16:9 landscape and a 9:16 vertical, and frame the photos for each.

FormatTypical useA landscape photo in this frame
16:9 (1,920 × 1,080)Website pages, YouTube videos, showroom screensFills the frame
9:16 (1,080 × 1,920)YouTube Shorts and other vertical feedsSits over a blurred fill, or loses most of its width to a crop
1:1Feed posts and marketplace galleriesSits over a blurred fill, or is cropped
4:5Feeds that allow a taller frameSits over a blurred fill, or is cropped

Cropping a typical landscape photo to 9:16 keeps only about a third of its width, and the subject is often what goes. Placing the whole photo over a blurred copy of itself keeps everything in view; the vertical slideshow guide compares the options.

On YouTube, square or vertical videos up to three minutes long are categorised as Shorts, and 16:9 videos are treated as regular uploads. For other platforms and paid placements, check the platform’s current guidance before you export.

How to make a slideshow video, step by step

Ten steps take a folder of photos to a published video.

  1. Set the job. One subject, one viewer, one length, the formats you need.
  2. Gather originals. Full-size files, 1,600 px or more on the long side, turned upright, duplicates removed.
  3. Order them. Establish, detail, close.
  4. Write the script. One sentence per photo, every fact checked against the source.
  5. Add cards. Title, information and closing cards.
  6. Record the voice. Yours or a synthetic one, with names and places pronounced correctly.
  7. Add motion and music. A slow zoom per photo, short fades, an optional quiet music bed.
  8. Caption it. From the final audio.
  9. Export each format. Check the vertical framing photo by photo.
  10. Review and publish. Watch once with sound and once without.

The same mistakes turn up in most slideshows:

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Too many photosEach one flashes past and the video runs longOne photo per sentence

Questions about making a slideshow video

How do I make a slideshow video from photos?

Choose a small set of sharp photos, put them in story order (establish, detail, close) and write one sentence of narration for each. Add cards, a slow zoom, optional quiet music and captions, then export a 16:9 and a 9:16 cut.

How many photos do I need for a 60-second slideshow video?

As a rule of thumb, 12 to 16 photos plus a title card and a closing card, with three to four seconds of narration per photo. For a 30-second video, 7 to 9 photos is usually enough.

How long should each photo stay on screen in a slideshow?

As long as the sentence that explains it, which as a rule of thumb is three to four seconds. Photos with text to read, such as a floor plan, need longer.

What resolution should photos be for a photo slideshow video?

A 1080p frame is 1,920 pixels on its long side, so originals of 1,600 px or more on the long side stay sharp. Photos from 1,000 px are usable but a little soft once enlarged, and smaller ones look low quality.

Can I create a slideshow video automatically from a listing or product page?

Yes. Videngine reads a listing or product page, takes its photos, writes and voices the narration, adds cards and captions, and renders 9:16 and 16:9 MP4s for review. One page or CSV row gives one video.
