Vertical slideshow: landscape photos in a 9:16 video

How to make a vertical slideshow from landscape photos: crop, letterbox or blurred fill, safe areas, captions and 1080×1920 for Reels, Shorts and TikTok.

The short answer

A vertical slideshow is a photo video in a 9:16 frame for Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and TikTok. Most business photos are landscape, so each one is either cropped to fill the frame, letterboxed on a plain colour, or placed over a blurred fill of itself. Crop only when the subject sits in the centre. Render at 1080×1920. Videngine uses the blurred fill and makes the 16:9 cut from the same source.

Three ways to fit a landscape photo in 9:16

There are three ways to put a landscape photo into a vertical frame, and each gives something up: picture, screen space or polish.

A 9:16 frame is tall and narrow, while most listing, product and venue photos are 3:2 or 4:3 the other way round. Pick one method and keep it throughout.

MethodWhat it keepsWhat it losesUse it when
Crop to fillA full-screen picture with no bandsThe sides: over 60% of a 3:2 photo's widthThe subject is narrow and centred
Letterbox on a plain colourThe whole photo, untouchedScreen space, and plain bands can look unfinishedFloor plans, documents, photos with text
Over a blurred fill of itselfThe whole photo, in a full frame of matching colourScreen space; the bands carry no detailMost landscape photos: rooms, vehicles, venues, line-ups

The blurred fill is a letterbox whose bands are an enlarged, blurred copy of the photo itself. A fourth option pans across a cropped photo, showing the full width over the scene but never at once; it suits panoramas and costs scene time. When cropping works and when it doesn't

Cropping works when the subject sits in the middle third with nothing important at the sides; it fails when the width is the point of the picture.

A 9:16 crop keeps a vertical strip from the middle of the photo. Here is how much survives, and what keeping the whole photo costs instead:

Photo shapeWidth kept by a 9:16 cropFrame height used if kept wholeOriginal needed to crop without enlarging
16:932%32% (1080×608)3414×1920
3:238%38% (1080×720)2880×1920
4:342%42% (1080×810)2560×1920
1:156%56% (1080×1080)1920×1920

So a crop suits photos whose middle strip tells the story: a portrait, a front door, a tall building, a product on a plain background. It also needs a far bigger original: 2,880 pixels wide for a 3:2 photo, against 1,080 to keep it whole.

It fails where the width carries the information. A car or boat shot side-on loses both ends, a living room loses the sense of space an estate agent is selling, and a product line-up loses the products at the edges. Subject-detection crops help with people, but no crop fits a wide subject into a narrow strip.

Safe areas, captions and buttons

Each app draws its interface over a vertical video, so captions, prices and logos must sit inside a safe area the overlays don't cover.

On Reels, Shorts and TikTok, the account name, post caption and buttons sit on top of the picture, mostly along the bottom and down the right-hand side. The safe area is what remains, and it moves between app versions and between posts and ads. TikTok's ads help centre, for example, says the safe zone for an in-feed ad depends on the video's dimensions and the ad caption's length, and provides safe zone files to lay over your edit (TikTok in-feed ad specs). For organic posts, check each platform's current guidance, or post a private test and watch it on a phone.

Caption placement follows from the layout:

  • Cropped photos: captions sit over the picture, so give them an outline or a solid box and keep them in the middle of the frame, above the bottom overlay.
  • Letterbox or blurred fill: put captions in the band just under the photo, off the subject.
  • Either way: two short lines at most, burned in if the narration carries the facts.

Resolution, and the 4:5 and 1:1 cuts

Render a vertical slideshow at 1080×1920 pixels, 9:16 at 1080p, and treat the photos, not the export setting, as the limit on sharpness.

That sits within each platform's limits. Instagram says a reel can be between 1.91:1 and 9:16, at a minimum of 720 pixels and 30 frames per second (Instagram: reel size and aspect ratios). YouTube says Shorts can be uploaded at up to 1080p (YouTube Help: upload Shorts), and TikTok's ads help recommends 9:16 at 540×960 or more. None of that sharpens a soft photo: a 900-pixel photo enlarged to fill the frame still looks like one.

Check the long side of each original. Videngine grades photos by it: 1,600 pixels or more is sharp, 1,000 to 1,599 is ok, and below 1,000 is low and flagged. The same thresholds work in any tool.

Shorter cuts suit landscape photos better:

Check each placement's current spec. YouTube counts square or vertical videos up to three minutes as Shorts and suggests a wider ratio such as 16:9 for long-form (YouTube Help: Shorts length); our page on YouTube videos from pages and data covers that side.

How to make a vertical slideshow from landscape photos

Choose the framing per photo, then set up the frame, safe areas and captions once and reuse them.

  1. Collect the originals. Use the largest files, not a website's resized copies.
  2. Sort the photos. Centred subjects can be cropped; wide ones need the whole photo. If most are wide, use the blurred fill throughout.
  3. Put them in order. Strongest photo first, then the details a buyer checks, then the call to action. Our guide on how to make a slideshow video covers order, timing and music.
  4. Set up the frame. 1080×1920 at 30 frames per second or more, with a safe-area guide on top.
  5. Add gentle motion. A slow zoom or pan stops a still looking frozen.
  6. Add narration and captions. Captions go in the band under the photo, clear of the overlays.
  7. Export the cuts. MP4 (H.264) at 1080×1920, then 4:5 or 1:1 for feeds and 16:9 for YouTube or your site, from the same edit.
  8. Test on a phone. Upload privately and check nothing important sits under the buttons.

When Videngine is the wrong tool

If your photos were shot vertically for social already, or you need one video once, a phone app or a simple editor will be quicker.

Photos taken on a phone held upright already fit 9:16, and for a single post the editor in the app you're posting to is faster than setting up a workspace.

Videngine also doesn't make slideshows cut to the beat of a song, collages or grids, or new motion from a still, which is AI image-to-video. Narration is English only, and there is no free plan. For a birthday or wedding slideshow, use a free consumer tool: the phone's photo app, iMovie, Google Photos or Canva.

Questions about vertical slideshows

What size is a vertical slideshow for Reels, Shorts and TikTok?

Make it 1080×1920 pixels, which is 9:16 at 1080p. All three platforms accept 9:16, and YouTube says Shorts can be uploaded at up to 1080p. Aim for original photos of at least 1,600 pixels on the long side.

How do I put landscape photos in a vertical video without cropping?

Fit each photo to the frame's width and fill the space above and below with a plain colour or a blurred, enlarged copy of the same photo. The blurred fill looks finished and keeps the whole photo. The cost is size: a 3:2 photo kept whole fills about 38% of the frame's height.

Can I use the same 9:16 slideshow for TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts?

Yes. One 1080×1920 MP4 can go to all three if captions and key text stay clear of each app's overlays, which differ. YouTube treats square or vertical videos up to three minutes long as Shorts.

Should a photo slideshow for Reels be 9:16 or 4:5?

Use 9:16 for Reels, the tallest ratio Instagram accepts for a reel, and make a separate 4:5 cut for feed posts. A 3:2 photo kept whole fills about 53% of a 4:5 frame's height, against 38% in 9:16.

Does a YouTube Shorts slideshow have to be vertical?

It has to be square or vertical. YouTube Help says videos with a square or vertical aspect ratio up to three minutes long are treated as Shorts, and suggests a wider ratio such as 16:9 for long-form videos. With landscape photos, make the Short and the long-form video as two cuts of one source.
