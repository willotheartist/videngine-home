Patient education videos from approved leaflets

Turn approved leaflets into patient education videos for waiting rooms, websites and YouTube: what to make, clinical sign-off, captions, privacy and updates.

The short answer

Patient education videos work best when they carry information a clinic has already approved in writing: how to prepare for an appointment, aftercare instructions in the clinic's own words, and how to use the practice's services. Use the approved leaflet as the script, word for word, have its owner sign off the finished video, caption it, and rebuild it whenever the leaflet changes. Videngine turns that text into a narrated, captioned MP4 that is reviewed before release.

What to turn into video, and what to leave with the clinician

Turn practical information that is the same for every patient into video, and leave anything that needs a clinician's judgement about one person in the consultation room.

TopicMake a video?Why
Preparing for an appointment: what to bring, when to arriveYesThe same for everyone booked for that appointment
Aftercare instructions, as written in the clinic's leafletYes, word for wordAlready approved; the video adds nothing new
Using the practice: booking, cancelling, parking, opening hours, out-of-hours contactsYesPractical, and it rarely changes
Whether something a patient notices after treatment is expected for themNoNeeds a clinician who knows the patient
Treatment choices, results, doses or one person's risksNoBelongs in a consultation
Claims about outcomes or comparisons with other providersNoThat is marketing, not patient information

Dental patient education videos show the split well. A practice might make one on preparing for a first visit, one on its payment plan, and one for each aftercare leaflet it already hands out. Whether a viewer's own symptoms are expected is a question for the dentist, not a video.

End every video by pointing to a person: the practice's phone number or contact route, exactly as printed on the leaflet.

Privacy: no patient images or data

Build these videos only from the practice's own text and images; no patient photographs, names, records or appointment details go in.

The ICO explains that the UK GDPR singles out health data as likely to be more sensitive and gives it extra protection. These videos need none of it, so exclude patients entirely rather than manage consent video by video.

  • No patient images: no before-and-after photos, which also drift towards outcome claims, and no shots of patients in the building.
  • No patient data in the source: paste the leaflet text, or give the address of a public page on the practice website, never a page behind a patient login.
  • No real cases as examples, even anonymised.
  • Staff photos: check that anyone shown is happy to appear.

Videngine sees only the text and images you give it. When it delivers to a Google Drive folder the practice owns, it sees only the files it creates there.

Clinical sign-off for patient education videos

Treat the video as a new format of the same approved document: the clinician who owns the leaflet signs off the finished render, not just the idea or the script.

  1. Pick the current leaflet. Confirm its version, approval date and owner.
  2. Supply the script word for word. Paste the approved text as the script instead of accepting a drafted one, and don't tidy it. Phrases that only work on paper, such as “see overleaf”, go back to the leaflet's owner to reword.
  3. Split long leaflets by heading, one short video per section, each still in the leaflet's exact words.
  4. Choose images from the practice's own material: the building, reception, signage, the logo, and any diagrams already approved in the leaflet. No patients.
  5. Set pronunciations for the practice name, clinicians' names, local streets and technical terms in the leaflet.
  6. Review the draft against the leaflet. Play it with sound, read every caption against the leaflet, and check each image sits with the right words. Any wording change goes back to step 2.
  7. Record the sign-off and release. Note who approved it, the leaflet version, the video version and the date, then publish.

Captions for waiting rooms, websites and YouTube

Caption every video: waiting-room screens play silent with captions burned into the picture, while website and YouTube versions are narrated and captioned.

The W3C describes captions as “a text version of the speech and non-speech audio information needed to understand the content”, and its captions guidance says they are required at WCAG Level A for prerecorded video with audio. Success criterion 1.2.2 exempts media that is a clearly labelled alternative to text, but captioning every video is simpler.

The W3C also says automatic captions need confirming as fully accurate. With a word-for-word script, captions should match the leaflet exactly. Our guide to video transcripts and captions covers burned-in and closed captions, SRT and WebVTT.

Patient education videos for the waiting room work best short, looped as a playlist. Check from the back row that every caption can be read.

YouTube's help centre says unlisted videos can be seen and shared by anyone with the link but don't show up in search results unless someone adds them to a public playlist; public videos can show up in search and related video lists. Unlisted suits a video linked from an appointment letter; public suits general information about the practice.

Keeping videos current when a leaflet is revised

Link every video to the leaflet version it was made from, and when the leaflet changes, rebuild it, sign it off again and replace it everywhere it plays.

In Videngine a rebuild is saved as a new version beside the original, so the record shows what patients saw. Republishing to a website page replaces the video in place; on YouTube and waiting-room screens, someone must remove the old file, so put that on the leaflet review checklist.

When a leaflet is withdrawn, withdraw its video the same day, everywhere it plays.

When to use a specialist producer or a clinician on camera

Use a specialist medical video producer when the video must show something happening, and put a clinician on camera when the message depends on who is saying it.

  • Demonstrating a physical technique: real footage, checked by the clinical team. Videngine does not generate footage.
  • Showing how something works inside the body: a medical animation studio. Videngine's motion is cards, photos and slow pan and zoom.
  • Patient stories: a producer who handles consent and filming.
  • A welcome from the team: a clinician on camera, even filmed simply. Videngine has no presenters or avatars.
  • Other languages: a translation and narration service working from an approved translated leaflet; Videngine narrates in English only.
  • Assessed staff training: an e-learning authoring tool for SCORM packages, quizzes or learner tracking, none of which Videngine makes.

Questions about patient education videos

What should a patient education video include?

Only information the clinic has already approved in writing that applies to everyone watching: preparing for an appointment, aftercare instructions in the clinic's own words, and how to use the practice. Anything that depends on the individual patient belongs in a consultation.

Can a dental practice play patient education videos in the waiting room?

Yes, if the content is practical and already approved, such as preparing for a first visit or how to book. Play them silent with captions burned into the picture, so the words reach everyone without sound.

Do patient information videos need captions?

Caption them all. WCAG 2.2 requires captions for prerecorded video with audio at Level A, and a silent waiting-room screen has no other way to deliver the words. Check the captions against the leaflet word for word.

Can AI write the script for a patient education video?

For patient information, don't let it. Supply the approved leaflet text as the script, word for word, and have the leaflet's owner sign off the finished video. A synthetic voice can still read it.

How do we update clinic videos when a leaflet changes?

Rebuild the video from the revised leaflet, sign it off again, and replace the old version everywhere it plays. Keep a register linking each video to the leaflet version it uses.
