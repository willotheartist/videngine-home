Course and lesson videos from your notes

Make lesson videos from written course notes: which parts suit narrated video, one objective per video, captions, a lesson structure and where to publish.

The short answer

Lesson videos work best when each one teaches a single objective in a few minutes: a hook, an explanation, a worked example and a recap, narrated and captioned. Your written course notes already hold most of that. Explanations, worked examples and recaps suit narrated video; demonstrations, discussion and feedback still need the teacher. Videngine turns each lesson's text into a narrated, captioned MP4 that you review before learners see it.

Which parts of a course suit narrated lesson videos

Explanations, worked examples, comparisons and recaps suit narrated video; anything that depends on the learner's response, or on seeing a physical skill, needs the teacher.

Course elementBest formatWhy
Concepts and definitionsNarrated videoAlready written; narration with an on-screen card carries them
Worked examples and calculationsNarrated videoEach figure appears as it is said, and learners can pause and replay
Comparisons: two methods, right and wrongNarrated videoSide-by-side scenes show the difference
Recaps and module summariesNarrated videoShort, easy to update and useful for revision
Software walkthroughsIt dependsA few screenshots work; a long click path needs a screen recording
Physical skills and demonstrationsThe teacherLearners need real footage of hands, tools and movement
Feedback and discussionThe teacherIt depends on what the learner says or does
Quizzes and assessmentYour LMSA video can ask a question; it can't mark the answer

Use video as a teaching tool for what stays the same each time it is taught. A definition doesn't change between cohorts; feedback on someone's work does.

A lesson video structure: one objective, four parts

Give each video one learning objective and build it in four parts: a hook, an explanation, a worked example and a recap.

One objective keeps the video short and cheap to correct; if it needs the word "and", it is probably two videos. Here is a sample from a small-business finance course: a 90-second lesson on gross margin.

PartTimeNarration (sample lines)On screen
Hook0–12 seconds“Two shops each take £40,000 a month. One keeps £14,000 after paying for its stock; the other keeps £6,000. Gross margin explains the gap.”Chapter title card: Gross margin
Explanation12–40 seconds“Gross margin is the share of sales left after paying for the goods you sold. Take cost of sales away from sales, then divide by sales.”Information card: (sales − cost of sales) ÷ sales
Worked example40–75 seconds“Shop A’s stock cost £26,000. £14,000 divided by £40,000 is 0.35: a gross margin of 35 per cent. Shop B’s stock cost £34,000, which leaves £6,000, or 15 per cent.”Side-by-side comparison: Shop A at 35%, Shop B at 15%
Recap75–90 seconds“Sales minus cost of sales, divided by sales. Next lesson: net margin, after overheads.”Information card repeating the formula

The worked example carries the lesson: use real numbers that add up and say each one as it appears. The recap restates the method and names the next lesson, which turns separate videos into a series. Our guide on how to write an explainer video script covers the same discipline.

Captions and transcripts for every lesson

Caption every lesson video and publish its transcript beside it.

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.2.2 requires captions for all prerecorded audio in synchronised media at Level A, so that people who are deaf or hard of hearing can follow the video. The W3C's guidance on transcripts adds that a text version also helps people who process text better than audio.

Adult learners also use transcripts to search for a term, copy a formula or read where they can't play sound. For a narrated lesson, the transcript is close to the approved script, so it costs little to publish.

Say what the screen shows, so the transcript misses nothing, and check technical terms in the captions before release. Our guide to video transcripts and captions covers SRT and WebVTT, burned-in versus closed captions, and accuracy checks.

Where to publish lesson videos: LMS, unlisted YouTube or a course website

Upload the MP4 to your LMS for enrolled learners, use unlisted YouTube when a link is enough, and publish to your course website when you want lessons to be found in search.

Unlisted YouTubeThe video, set to unlistedFree material shared by linkAnyone with the link can watch and reshare it
Public YouTubeA few taster lessonsBringing new learners to the courseTitles and descriptions written for search

Unlisted is not private. YouTube's help page on privacy settings says anyone with the link can see and share an unlisted video, though it won't show in YouTube's search results (unless added to a public playlist) or on your channel's Videos tab. Keep paid lessons in your LMS.

Creating educational videos for YouTube in public is a different job: a taster lesson is marketing for the course, so it needs a title written for search and a route to the full course. See building a YouTube channel for business from your website.

From lesson notes to a video series: the workflow

Making educational videos from existing notes is mostly planning: build the series from your objectives, prove one pilot lesson, then make the rest the same way.

  1. List the objectives. Write one per line for the module. Each line becomes one video.
  2. Sort the material. Mark each lesson: narrated video, teacher on camera, screen recording or LMS activity.
  3. Shape each script. Cut the notes for each objective into hook, explanation, worked example and recap, then read it aloud.
  4. Gather the visuals. Collect diagrams, screenshots and photos you have the right to use.
  5. Make one pilot. Show it to two or three learners. Check pace, pronunciation, captions and whether the example lands.
  6. Fix the house style. Settle the voice, course terms and colours, so module six matches module one.
  7. Produce the series. Make the remaining lessons in a batch and check each one against the notes, figure by figure.
  8. Publish and maintain. Name files by module and lesson (M02-L03-gross-margin.mp4), publish with captions and transcripts, and rebuild one lesson when its notes change.

The same pattern applies to turning a written article into a video.

Questions about lesson videos

How long should a lesson video be?

Long enough to teach one objective and no longer, which for most written lessons is a few minutes of narration. If the script needs two worked examples for two different ideas, split it into two videos.

Can I turn my course notes into lesson videos without filming?

Yes, for explanations, worked examples, comparisons and recaps. Narration over cards, diagrams and screenshots carries those without a camera. Demonstrations of physical skills and anything that responds to the learner still need the teacher.

What should I look for in an educational video maker for courses?

Captions on every video, a script you can edit or supply word for word, one look across the series, and MP4 files your LMS accepts. Check that you can update one lesson alone, and that you keep the files if you stop paying.

Should I put my course videos on YouTube?

Put a few taster lessons on YouTube publicly to bring people to the course, and keep paid lessons in your LMS. Unlisted videos can be watched and reshared by anyone with the link.

Do lesson videos need captions?

Yes. WCAG 2.2 requires captions for prerecorded video with audio at Level A, the minimum level of conformance. Publish a transcript as well, so learners can search the lesson and revise from the text.
