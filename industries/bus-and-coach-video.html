Bus and coach video: a narrated video for every bus for sale

Bus sales video for every bus and coach in stock: narrated, captioned cuts of seats, wheelchair access, luggage and mileage, each fleet unit kept distinct.

The short answer

A bus and coach video is a short narrated walkaround of one bus for sale, built from the photos and specification on its listing: seats and layout, wheelchair access, luggage, engine and gearbox, mileage, air conditioning and condition. Buses are high-value purchases, buyers are often far away, and fleets are full of near-identical units, so each bus needs its own video with its own stock number and photos. Videngine reads each listing or stock row and renders captioned 9:16 and 16:9 cuts for review.

Bus sales video for every unit, not only the best coach on the yard

Buses and coaches suit a video per unit because each is a large purchase, the buyer is often far away, and it usually sits beside near-identical units where small differences decide the sale.

  • High value. An operator buying a used coach wants to see the unit they would pay for, not a sister vehicle or a brochure.
  • Buyers far away. Operators often buy from other regions or abroad, and may agree terms before anyone travels. A video of that bus, saloon, lift and odometer included, tells them whether the journey is worth making.
  • Near-identical fleets. Ten single-deckers from one operator share a model, year and livery. What differs is seats, lift or ramp, mileage, condition and price: what a per-unit video puts on screen.

The case for a video per stock unit, the walkaround order and the price policy are in the car dealer video guide; this page covers what changes for coaches, service buses, minibuses and school buses. Vans and trucks have their own guide: van and truck video.

Seating codes and stated capacity

Read capacity exactly as the listing gives it. Many UK listings use a seating code such as C53F, a coach with 53 seats and a front entrance. Show the code on the facts card and set its spoken form in the lexicon, so the voice never spells it out. Where a listing gives seated and standing capacity, or a school bus listing gives more than one passenger figure, show each with its label, never only the larger.

Wheelchair access and regulatory wording

Describe accessibility equipment exactly as the listing does, and never say a bus complies with an accessibility regulation unless the listing says so.

The listing saysThe video saysThe video does not say
Rear wheelchair lift, one positionA rear wheelchair lift and one wheelchair position“Fully accessible”, “wheelchair friendly”
Manual ramp at the centre doorA manual ramp at the centre door“Powered ramp”, or “low floor” unless listed
Lift removed, seats fittedThe seat count, with no access sceneAnything about wheelchair access
A compliance statementThose words, unchangedA date or certificate the listing does not give
Nothing about regulationsThe equipment only“PSVAR compliant”, “ADA compliant”

In Great Britain, GOV.UK's guidance on the Public Service Vehicles Accessibility Regulations 2000 (PSVAR) says they apply to new buses and coaches with a capacity exceeding 22 passengers used to provide a local or scheduled service, in England, Scotland and Wales; Northern Ireland has its own regulations. Its guidance for manufacturers and operators says vehicles the regulations apply to are issued with an accessibility certificate. Whether a used bus must comply depends on the work its buyer puts it to, which the video cannot know.

In the US, ADA.gov describes the Americans with Disabilities Act as a federal civil rights law prohibiting discrimination against people with disabilities, and lists intercity and charter buses among its examples of privately operated transit. The US Access Board says vehicles covered by the ADA must comply with standards issued by the Department of Transportation. A lift is equipment; “ADA compliant” is a claim. Say the first, and the second only when the listing does.

Put “wheelchair friendly” and similar on the banned-phrases list. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.

Fleet listings: making each unit distinguishable

When an operator sells a batch of similar buses, each video must be unmistakably one unit: its stock number on screen, its own photos and its own mileage.

Illustrative only: a fictional disposal of four single-deckers of one model and year.

Stock numberMileageSeats and accessCondition as listed
BC1041384,200 miles41 seats, ramp, one wheelchair positionScuffed nearside skirt panel
BC1042401,950 miles41 seats, ramp, one wheelchair positionWorn rear seat moquette
BC1043366,480 miles37 seats, ramp, wheelchair position and buggy spaceClean
BC1044420,310 miles41 seats, rampRamp not working, as listed
  • Stock number on screen. In the opening and closing scenes and the file name, for example BC1043_16x9_v1.mp4.
  • The unit's own photos. A listing that borrows a sister vehicle's photos makes a video of the wrong bus. Hold it until its own photos arrive, and check yard shots for another unit's plate.
  • Its own mileage and condition. The facts card reads from that unit's row and must agree with its odometer photo. A fault, such as BC1044's ramp, gets its own scene.
  • Review every unit. Near-identical buses are where a copied mileage slips through; check each facts card against that unit's photos.
  • Titles that differ. On YouTube, put the stock number and the distinguishing fact in each title. YouTube's spam policy does not allow automated tools to be used to flood the platform with repetitive content, so publish a selection there and keep every unit's video on its listing.

From bus inventory to approved videos

The workflow takes your listings or stock file to two reviewed cuts per bus, and keeps each in step with the price and the sale.

  1. Set up the workspace. Choose vehicles and equipment as the business type, and build one scene set per type you sell (coaches, service buses, minibuses, school buses), each with its own facts card and photo order.
  2. Seed the lexicon and voice rules. Add builders, chassis, seating codes and depot towns, plus banned phrases. Set a target length per scene set: a coach with lockers, toilet and lift needs longer than a minibus. A video up to two minutes counts as one.
  3. Pick the stock. Have Videngine read your sitemap and pick bus listings, paste one listing URL, or send a CSV or JSON row per unit from your stock system or an operator's disposal list; batch runs start on Growth (see bulk video creation from a CSV).
  4. Review with defect codes. Every video is reviewed in the studio before it goes out; a rejection carries a code that shows where the fix belongs (sign-off and defect codes are on Scale and Custom). A workspace can require the approver to be someone other than the builder.
  5. Render. Each bus gets a 9:16 and a 16:9 MP4 up to 1080p, captions burned in or as .srt, named by stock number.
  6. Keep it in step. When the price changes, update the listing and render again: the rebuild is saved as _v2 beside the original, free within the same month. When the bus sells, retire its video everywhere it was published.

Where bus and coach videos go

Put the landscape cut on the bus's own listing page, publish a selection to YouTube, and give the sales team a Drive folder of both cuts.

  • The listing page. The 16:9 cut sits beside the photos; see video on the vehicle detail page. On a static HTML site built from a GitHub repository, Videngine can publish into the page with player, transcript, captions and VideoObject markup.
  • YouTube. The landscape cut and the vertical Short, each with its own title, description, hashtags and tags (Scale, rolling out). See YouTube videos for businesses.
  • A Drive folder for the sales team. Approved cuts land in a Google Drive folder the business owns, with a sheet listing every bus and links to both cuts (Growth, rolling out), so a salesperson can send a buyer abroad the right video by stock number.
  • Portals and social. Check whether each bus sales portal takes a video file or a YouTube link; see vehicle video for portals and dealer social video.
  • Your own systems. The read-only API (Growth) looks up the approved video for a bus by stock number.

Questions bus and coach dealers ask about listing video

How do I make a video for every bus and coach in stock?

Start from the listings you already publish, or a CSV row per unit. Videngine turns each into a narrated, captioned 9:16 and 16:9 cut named by stock number, reviewed in the studio before it goes out.

What should a used bus or minibus video include?

Chassis, body builder, model and year; seats and layout; wheelchair lift or ramp and positions; luggage; engine, emission standard and gearbox; mileage; air conditioning; destination displays; condition; price policy, depot and stock number, each shown in a photo of this unit.

Can a wheelchair accessible bus video say the bus is PSVAR or ADA compliant?

Only if the listing says so, in its own words. Otherwise the video names the equipment, such as a rear lift and one wheelchair position, and makes no regulatory claim.

How do I tell apart videos of identical buses from one fleet?

Give each unit its stock number on screen and in the file name, its own photos, and its own mileage and condition on the facts card. Hold any unit with a sister vehicle's photos, and review every unit.

Can Videngine show the bus driving or the lift working?

No. AI writes the script and the voice, but every picture comes from the listing's photos or footage you supply. To show the bus on the road or the lift cycling, film it, then supply the footage or publish it alongside the listing video.
