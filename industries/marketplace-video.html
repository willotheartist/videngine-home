Marketplace listing video: a video for every listing on the platform

The short answer

A marketplace listing video is a short narrated video that the platform makes from a listing's own photos and structured data, instead of waiting for the seller to film one. Because the platform already holds both, every listing that passes basic quality checks can have one. Videngine reads each listing page or feed row, writes a script from the listing's fields and renders captioned vertical and landscape cuts in batches, each reviewed before it goes live.

Marketplace listing video: why the platform should make it

The platform should make the video because it already holds everything a listing video needs, and most sellers will never film one.

A marketplace carries thousands of listings across vehicles, boats, property and equipment. Filming is real work for a private seller, so seller-shot video tends to cover a few listings, not the catalogue. Yet every listing already has photos, a location and structured fields such as make, model and year. That is enough for a narrated, captioned video, so video for online marketplaces can come from two places.

Content rights and seller trust

Before you make videos from sellers' photos and descriptions, confirm your terms allow it, give sellers a way out, and keep the narration to what the listing says.

  • Check your terms. Does the licence sellers grant over their photos and text cover making a video and publishing it where you plan to: the listing, a YouTube channel, promotion? If unclear, take legal advice and update the terms before the backfill. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.
  • Tell sellers first that each video is built only from their own listing.
  • Offer an opt-out and a flag. Give an opt-out per listing and per account, and a report button that takes a video down while it is fixed. Carry opt-outs into the feed.
  • Narrate only what the listing says. Scripts come from the listing's fields, and brand voice rules add banned phrases, so the video claims nothing about condition or value that the seller did not.
  • Know who owns what. The platform owns the rendered files and scripts; the photos inside remain subject to your terms with the seller.

Quality gates and priority: which listings get a video first

Hold back any listing whose video would be thin, wrong or short-lived, then order the rest so the backfill reaches the listings that matter first.

Run the gates on the feed before anything renders, so a held listing never becomes a counted video. Tune the thresholds per category.

Then order what passes:

  • Newest first. The backfill works backwards from today and meets the daily flow.
  • Most viewed. Rank by listing views from your own analytics.
  • Category by category. Start where photos are strongest and the template is proven.
  • Paid placement. If video joins a featured or dealer tier, those listings go first.

Backfill and daily flow: the rollout workflow

Run two flows: a one-off backfill of live stock in prioritised batches, and a daily flow that picks up new and changed listings.

Listing video at scale starts with a page list. Videngine can read your sitemap and list its listing pages; your team picks which to render, in batches. Under the sitemaps.org protocol, each sitemap file holds at most 50,000 URLs, larger sites use a sitemap index, and lastmod gives the date a page was last modified, which helps a daily run find edited listings. To send data rather than pages, use a CSV or JSON feed; see bulk video creation from a CSV and what to check in a video automation API.

  1. Pilot one category. Take about 50 listings, approve one template, set the price policy and seed the pronunciation lexicon with makes, models, builders and place names.
  2. Build the page list from the sitemap or a feed with listing reference, category, photo count, dates and views.
  3. Apply the gates and record why each listing was held.
  4. Batch the backfill. Work in priority order, in batches reviewers can clear in a day. Batch jobs queue and run unattended.
  5. Render both cuts. Each listing gives a 9:16 and a 16:9 cut, named by listing reference. In the vertical cut, a landscape photo sits over a blurred fill of itself, so the item is not cropped out.
  6. Review, then deliver. Use the review model and delivery routes below.
  7. Switch on the daily flow. Each day, gate and render new and changed listings. After a price change, re-render: the rebuild is saved as _v2 beside the original, free within the same month.
  8. Retire. When a listing sells or expires, remove its video wherever it was published.

Review at scale without slowing the queue

Every video is reviewed in the studio before it goes out; at marketplace volume, the choice is how deep each check goes.

While a template is new, review every video in full: once with sound and once without, every fact checked against the listing. Once rejections are rare, give every video a quick check (opening facts, price, names, the vertical cut on a phone) and fully review a sample from each batch. A defect in the sample returns that template to full review.

A workspace can require that the person who approves a video is not the person who built it. Every rejection carries a defect code that points to a fix upstream.

The video ledger records each version with its review status, builder, reviewer and any defect code, and exports to CSV: join it to your listing table to find live listings without an approved video. Custom rollouts can add a coverage report: how much of the live inventory has an approved video and what is waiting on whom.

Delivery and cost at platform scale

A platform at this volume usually fits the Custom plan, from 1,500 videos a month, which delivers videos straight into listings; published Scale rates make a useful benchmark.

Where the videos go

  • Into the listing. On Custom, each approved video is delivered into its listing. Making it the first gallery item is an option and a product decision; test it on your own traffic.
  • A marketplace YouTube channel. A channel Owner connects it once; approved videos are queued and paced within YouTube's daily upload allowance, with privacy and synthetic-content disclosure as settings. YouTube's spam policy does not allow automated tools to flood the platform with repetitive content, so publish featured listings, not the catalogue. See YouTube videos for businesses.
  • Google Drive and a Sheet. Files land in a folder the business owns, with a Google Sheet listing every item and links to both cuts and the listing.

On search: Google's video documentation lists a product page with a 360 video among pages that are not watch pages, and requires a watch page for its video features. Plan listing video for buyers on the page.

Questions about marketplace video

How can a marketplace add a video to every listing?

Generate it on the platform from each listing's photos and fields instead of asking sellers to film. Set quality gates, backfill live stock in batches, then run a daily flow for new listings. Videngine renders a captioned vertical and landscape cut per listing, each reviewed before it goes out.

Can a classifieds site make videos from sellers' photos?

Only if its terms allow it, so check the licence sellers grant over their photos and text, and take legal advice if unclear. Then tell sellers, offer an opt-out and a way to flag a problem, and keep the narration to what the listing says.

How much do automated listing videos cost at marketplace scale?

On launch pricing, Scale is $599 a month for 750 videos, with extras at $0.80 each; same-month re-renders are free. From 1,500 videos a month, the Custom plan sets a volume rate and adds delivery straight into listings. Backfills are quoted once.

Which listings should not get a video?

Hold back listings with too few photos, missing required fields, duplicate or stock photos, or days left to run. Price-on-application listings use a template without a price scene. Opted-out sellers are skipped.

Are automated listing videos AI-generated footage?

Not with Videngine. AI writes the script and the narration voice, but the pictures are the listing's own photos, framed for each format with slow pan and zoom. The video only shows what the seller photographed.
