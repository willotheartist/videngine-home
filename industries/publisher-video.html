Publisher video: turn articles into videos at newsroom scale

How digital publishers, newsrooms and trade titles turn published articles into narrated, captioned videos every day: which stories suit video, image rights, desk sign-off and corrections.

The short answer

Publisher video is video a publication makes from its own journalism: a published article becomes a short narrated explainer for YouTube, Shorts and the article page. It works best for evergreen guides, explainers and settled data stories, using owned images, with a desk editor signing off each video. Videngine lists new articles from your sitemap and, for each one the desk picks, writes the script, narrates and captions it, and renders landscape and vertical cuts.

Which articles deserve a publisher video

Articles that stay true for months and explain one thing clearly make the best publisher video; fast-moving news and opinion make the worst.

Article typeFit for template videoWhy
Evergreen guideStrongAnswers a question readers keep asking, and stays accurate.
ExplainerStrongAlready a question and an answer; its sections map onto chapter cards and information cards.
Review or comparisonGood, with careSide-by-side scenes suit it. Keep the verdict attributed to the reviewer, clear the product images and leave out prices that will date.
Data storyStrong once figures are finalEach figure becomes a card with its source named. Weak while the data are provisional.
Breaking newsWeak while the story movesA render fixes one version of the facts. Wait until the article settles, or plan to re-render as it changes.
OpinionRarelyThe value is the writer's own voice; a synthetic voice reading a column sounds like nobody. If one deserves video, use the columnist's own recorded reading.

This page covers article to video for publishers as a daily operation. For the craft of adapting one piece, from choosing its question to rewriting prose for the ear, read article to video: turn a blog post into a clear explainer.

Clear image rights before you render

A photo licensed for an article page is not necessarily licensed for video, YouTube or social feeds, so check each image's terms before it goes into a render.

Licences can be limited by medium, platform, territory or period. Ask the picture desk before the batch, not after publication.

Image sourceCheck before renderingDefault
Staff photographyHouse policy on reuse across formatsUse
Commissioned freelanceWhether the commission covers video and third-party platformsUse only if covered
Agency or wireWhether the licence covers video, YouTube and social useLeave out unless covered
Handout or PR imagesThe stated terms and the required creditUse as stated, with the credit
Embedded social postsWhether the account holder has agreed to reuse in videoLeave out, or ask the account holder
Reader-submittedWhat the submitter agreed toLeave out unless the terms cover video
In-house chartsThe data source and its creditRebuild as information cards

Videngine pulls images from the article page by default; when an article mixes owned and licensed pictures, supply only the cleared ones alongside it in a CSV or JSON payload. A reviewer who finds an image that should not be there rejects the video with the photo defect code. This is practical advice, not legal advice: your rights desk or lawyer has the final word.

Hold the video to the article's standards

The video is another version of the article, so it says only what the article says and goes through the same sign-off.

Editorial video production at volume fails in predictable places: a dropped qualifier, an overstated title, a mispronounced name, an uncorrected video.

  • Same claims, nothing new: check every figure, name, quote and attribution against the published text. A dropped “alleged” changes the story.
  • Titles are headlines: Videngine writes a YouTube title for each cut, with a different angle for the Short. Check both as you would the article's headline.
  • The desk editor signs off: the editor who owns the article approves its video. A workspace can require that the approver is not the person who built it.
  • House style in the voice rules: a pronunciation lexicon gets the names of people, places and organisations right every time; banned phrases enforce the style guide.
  • Captions match the audio: they are transcribed from the finished narration.

Corrections

When an article is corrected, re-render its video from the corrected text and replace it wherever it was published: upload the new cut, make the old one private, swap the embed and note the correction in the description. The rebuild is saved as a new version, such as _v2, beside the original; the video ledger records who built and reviewed each version and exports to CSV for your corrections log.

Disclosure

Tell viewers the narration is synthetic and the script was drafted with AI, the same way on every video.

YouTube's help page on disclosing AI content requires creators to disclose when AI is used to meaningfully alter or generate photorealistic content, such as making a real person appear to say something they did not. It lists AI help with a script or title, and caption creation, as needing no disclosure, and does not specifically address synthetic narration.

A newsroom should aim above the platform minimum. A line in every description, such as “Narrated with a synthetic voice. Script drafted with AI and checked by our editors.”, is plain and consistent. Never present the voice as a named reporter. Synthetic-content disclosure is a setting when Videngine publishes to YouTube; set it once.

Set a daily cadence from the sitemap

Run publisher video as a daily batch: each morning, take the previous day's new evergreen articles from the sitemap and send them through as one job.

Example: a 60-second explainer from a data story

A data story makes a good 60-second explainer when each scene carries one figure and the caveat stays on screen.

This plan is illustrative, for a hypothetical data story on where a county's new homes were built last year, with a table by district and a note that the figures count completions, not permissions.

From sitemap to published video

Eight steps take an article from the sitemap to YouTube and back onto its own page; three need an editor's judgement.

  1. Set up the workspace once. Choose the media and publishing workspace, set typefaces, colours, logo, safe areas and motion as brand tokens, and add the pronunciation lexicon, banned phrases and a target length.
  2. Pick the batch from the sitemap. Videngine lists the site's articles; the desk editor picks those published since the last batch that pass the fit table.
  3. Clear the images. Keep owned images; where an article mixes sources, supply the cleared ones alongside it.
  4. Draft the scripts. Videngine reads each article in a headless browser, drops navigation and cookie banners, and writes a script, a timed scene plan and YouTube metadata. How URL-to-video reading works covers this in detail.
  5. Check against the article. Compare figures, names, quotes, attributions and both titles with the published text. Accept the script, edit a line or supply your own.
  6. Render. Narration is recorded, captions are transcribed from the finished audio, and both cuts render to MP4.
  7. Sign off. The desk editor reviews each video in the studio; rejections carry a defect code, such as pronunciation, photo or brand.
  8. Publish and file. Queue approved videos to YouTube with privacy and disclosure set, deliver the MP4s to your Drive folder for the embeds, and the ledger records the version.

Steps 2, 5 and 7 need judgement; the batch runs unattended in between.

When Videngine is the wrong tool

Videngine is the wrong tool for any video whose value is footage, a person or a moment as it happens.

  • Live news: a story told as it unfolds needs a live production or streaming setup.
  • On-the-ground footage: a reporter's own video needs a video journalist and a timeline editor such as Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve. Videngine can narrate over footage you supply, but it does not shoot or cut it.
  • Documentary: needs a production team, original footage and an edit suite.
  • Presenter-led shows: need a studio and the host. Videngine has no avatars or on-camera presenters; for synthetic presenters, see how presenter-led tools compare.
  • Podcast highlights: finding the best moments in a long recording is a job for a clipping tool built for audio and video. Videngine starts from text, data or a web page.

Publisher video questions

What is publisher video?

Publisher video is video a publication makes from its own journalism, usually short explainers for YouTube, Shorts and the article page. Made from templates, one article becomes a narrated, captioned video in landscape and vertical cuts, checked against the article before it is published.

Can news video automation handle breaking news?

With care, and usually not while the story is still moving. A rendered video fixes one version of the facts, so wait until the article is stable, or plan to re-render as it changes. Live coverage needs a live production setup instead.

Can we use agency photos when we turn articles into videos at scale?

Only if the licence covers video and the platforms you publish on. A licence for the article page does not necessarily extend to YouTube or social feeds. Check each agency or contributor licence before rendering, and use owned images where you can.

Do publishers need to disclose AI narration?

YouTube requires disclosure of photorealistic content made or altered with AI, and lists AI help with scripts and captions as needing none; its page does not specifically address synthetic narration. Label it anyway: say the voice is synthetic and the script was drafted with AI and checked by editors, the same way on every video.

How many articles a day can a newsroom turn into videos?

Review time sets the limit, not rendering. A one-minute 1080p video typically renders in a few minutes, so the real question is how many videos your desk editors can check properly. Plans run from 50 to 750 videos a month, with Custom from 1,500.
