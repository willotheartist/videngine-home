AI voiceover for video: voices, pronunciation and disclosure

AI voiceover for video: pick a voice, fix pronunciation with a lexicon, write scripts for the ear, get consent before voice cloning and disclose where needed.

The short answer

AI voiceover for video is narration made by text-to-speech from a written script. Three things decide whether it sounds right: a voice chosen for pace, clarity and fit with the audience; a pronunciation lexicon so names, models, places, acronyms and numbers are said correctly; and a script written for the ear. Get written consent before cloning anyone's voice, and disclose where platforms ask. Videngine narrates in English with a house or brand voice and a per-brand lexicon.

How to choose a voice for video narration

Whichever voice over maker or text-to-speech tool you use for video, choose a voice by listening to your own script, not the vendor's demo line: pace, clarity and brand fit matter more than how human a sample sounds.

Write a test paragraph with a model name, a place, an acronym, a price and a date. Render it in three or four voices and play each on a phone speaker, which is where most people will hear it, as well as headphones.

Listen forGood signWarning sign
PaceA real pause at each full stopLists blur into one long word
ClarityConsonants survive a phone speaker“Fifteen” and “fifty” sound alike
EmphasisStress lands on the new informationEvery sentence ends on the same falling note
Brand fitSounds like someone who could work for youTrailer drama for a spreadsheet tool
AudienceAn accent your buyers expectLocal place names sound foreign

As a rule of thumb, conversational narration runs at roughly 140 to 160 words a minute, slower for dense technical material. At about 150 words a minute, a 30-second cut holds around 75 words and a 2-minute explainer around 300. If the script runs long, cut words rather than speeding up the voice.

One house voice or one per brand. A single brand is usually best served by one house voice: viewers hear the same narrator across a series, and every re-render sounds the same. Separate voices make sense when one team publishes for brands with different audiences, such as a dealer group with a premium marque and a value outlet. Mixing voices within one brand's channel sounds like several companies.

Match the voice to the audience. Use a British English voice for UK buyers and an American one for US buyers. Trade buyers want a plain, brisk read.

Fix pronunciation with a lexicon

Text-to-speech guesses at any word it does not know, so names, model codes, places, acronyms and numbers go wrong; a pronunciation lexicon fixes each one once by mapping the written form to the spoken form.

A speech engine turns spelling into sound with a dictionary and rules. Surnames, model codes and local place names are often missing from it. Acronyms are ambiguous: API is spelled out, NASA is said as a word.

The W3C Pronunciation Lexicon Specification defines a lexicon as a mapping between words, their written forms and their pronunciations, with an alias for acronym expansions: its own example reads W3C as World Wide Web Consortium. Most voice tools accept a respelling or a phonetic transcription; respellings are easier for a marketing team to maintain.

Numbers, dates, prices and units should be said the way people say them, which often depends on context the engine cannot see.

Recurring terms such as brand names, models and places belong in the lexicon, so every video gets them right. One-off numbers and dates go into the script already written as words.

Write the script for the ear

AI narration is heard once, at the speaker's pace, so write short sentences with one idea each and leave tables and lists on screen.

  • One idea per sentence. A listener cannot re-read a clause they missed.
  • Subject first. “The flat has two bedrooms and a balcony” beats “Offering two bedrooms and a balcony, this flat…”
  • No tables read aloud. Show the specification on screen and say the one or two figures that matter.
  • No brackets, slashes or “respectively”. None of them survive being spoken.
  • Read it aloud before rendering. If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long.

Voice cloning: get consent in writing

If a business wants narration in a real person's voice, such as a founder or spokesperson, get that person's written consent for specific uses, keep it on file, and follow the voice provider's own rules.

Then read the provider's terms, which can be stricter than your own. At the time of writing, the ElevenLabs professional voice cloning guide says it currently only allows you to clone your own voice, not someone else's even with their consent, and each professional clone is verified as belonging to you. Other providers set their own rules; keep a copy with the consent record.

Disclosing a synthetic voice

YouTube asks creators to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content, including making a real person appear to say something they did not; a synthetic narrator reading your own script is not on its list, but a cloned voice of a real person needs more thought.

YouTube's help page on disclosing use of GenAI content requires disclosure when AI meaningfully alters or generates photorealistic content: making a real person appear to say or do something they did not do, altering footage of a real event or place, generating a realistic scene that did not occur, or creating music that is the main focus of the video. It lists as not needing disclosure: AI help with a script, caption creation, voice or audio repair, and cloning one's own voice to create voiceovers or dubs.

The setting is “AI use”, in the Attributes section when you upload in YouTube Studio. The page says disclosing will not limit a video's audience or its eligibility to earn money.

Captions follow the voice

Make captions from the finished audio, not from the script, so the words and timings on screen match what the viewer hears.

Script and narration drift apart: a human reader rephrases a line, someone trims a sentence after approval. Captions split from the script carry every mismatch, and their timings are guesses. Transcribing the final audio gives matching words, timed from the voice itself.

Check the caption file for names the lexicon fixed, since a transcript can misspell a model the voice said correctly. Then publish captions as a track as well as burning them in. Questions about AI voiceover

What should I listen for in a voice over maker?

Listen to your own script, not the demo line. Check pace, clarity on a phone speaker, where the stress falls, and whether the voice suits your brand and your buyers' accent. Include a name, an acronym, a price and a date in the test.

How do I make text to speech pronounce names correctly?

Add a pronunciation lexicon entry that maps the written form to the spoken form, such as Worcester to wuss-ter. Put recurring names in the lexicon so every video gets them right. Write one-off numbers and dates into the script as words.

Do I need consent to clone someone's voice for a video?

Yes. Get written consent covering the provider, permitted uses, duration and withdrawal, and keep it on file. Also follow the provider's terms: ElevenLabs' professional voice cloning guide, for example, currently only allows you to clone your own voice.

Do I have to disclose an AI voice on YouTube videos?

YouTube requires disclosure for realistic AI content, including making a real person appear to say something they did not. It lists cloning your own voice for voiceovers as not needing disclosure and does not address a stock synthetic narrator directly. If unsure, set AI use to Yes when you upload.

Can Videngine use my own voice or other languages?

You can upload your own recorded audio instead of the synthetic voice, and captions are transcribed from it. The Scale plan adds a cloned voice, created only with the speaker’s written consent, and narration is in English only.
