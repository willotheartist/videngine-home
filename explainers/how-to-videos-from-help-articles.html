How-to videos from help articles: a tutorial for every article

Use a how-to video maker to turn help articles into short tutorials: which articles suit video, steps as scenes, keeping videos current and embedding them.

The short answer

A how-to video maker turns a help article into a short tutorial: one step per scene, the article's own screenshot on screen, narration that says exactly what to click, and captions that match. Start with task articles of three to eight steps on a screen that rarely changes. Videngine reads each article from your sitemap, drafts the script and scene plan, and renders a narrated, captioned MP4 for review, ready to sit at the top of the article.

Which help articles suit video

Task articles with a handful of steps on a stable screen suit video best; reference pages, policies and long branching procedures do not.

Sort by article type before traffic: a popular table of settings still makes a poor video.

Article typeSuits video?Why
Task how-to (“How to export invoices”)YesOne goal, a few clicks, a visible result. The numbered steps map straight onto scenes.
Getting started and setupYesNew users follow more easily once they have seen the screen.
Concept or “what is” articleYes, as an explainerNo clicks to show. Chapter cards and narration carry the idea.
Troubleshooting with one known fixSometimesWorks when the symptom is easy to show; a decision tree of causes does not.
Long procedure with branchesSplit it firstA viewer cannot skip to step 9 “if you use single sign-on”. Make one video per path.
Reference: settings lists, API fields, limitsNoPeople scan and copy from these. A video makes them wait.
Policies, billing terms, legal wordingNoExact wording matters and should be read, not heard.
Feature in beta or about to changeNot yetThe video will be out of date before many people watch it.

A workable test: if you can name the finished state in one sentence (“the invoices are downloaded as a CSV”) and a new customer could reach it in under two minutes, it is a tutorial. Concept articles belong with the explainer video maker workflow.

How steps become scenes

Each numbered step becomes one scene: the step's screenshot on screen, a sentence or two of narration that names the click, and captions that match the narration word for word.

Videngine reads the article in a headless browser and keeps the text and images, so the screenshots already in the article come across with the steps. It writes a script and timed scene plan to accept, edit or replace. The guide to writing an explainer video script covers the wording in more depth.

In the articleIn the video
Title (“How to export invoices”)A chapter title card naming the task
Opening line: what you will achieveThe first sentence of narration, over the starting screen
Each numbered step and its screenshotOne screenshot scene with a slow zoom, narrated with the click
Note or warning (“Only admins see this menu”)An information card, held long enough to read
Old setting beside newA side-by-side comparison scene
Final resultThe finished screen, then a closing card pointing back to the article

Narration that says the click follows four rules:

  • Use the exact label. “Select Export”, not “hit the button at the top”.
  • Say where it is when the screen is busy. “Export, at the top right of the invoice list.”
  • One action per sentence. The scene changes when the action does.
  • Say the result. “The file downloads as a CSV”, so the viewer knows the step worked.

Put “simply” and “just” on the brand's banned phrases list; a step that is simple for the writer may not be for the person stuck on it. Captions are transcribed back from the finished audio, so they match what was said, and are burned in for people watching muted. If a control is small, crop the screenshot to its panel before rendering, especially for a vertical cut.

Keeping tutorials current when the interface changes

Treat the article as the source of truth: when the interface changes, update the article and its screenshots, then re-render the video from the article.

The video is rendered from the article, not cut on a timeline, so there is no project file to reopen. Videngine saves the rebuild as a new version, such as _v2, beside the original, and the video ledger records each version. Re-rendering the same video in the same month is free.

What changedWhat to do
A label was renamedCorrect the article and screenshot, then re-render
A control movedReplace the screenshot and the “where it is” sentence, then re-render
A step was added or removedEdit the steps and review the new scene plan
The feature was retiredTake the video down with the article

Record which videos show which screen, so a release that changes a page tells support what to rebuild. The SaaS video guide covers capturing and naming screenshots for re-renders.

Embedding the video in the article itself

Put the video at the top of the article it was made from, above the written steps, and keep the text: readers scan and copy from it, and search engines and AI assistants read it.

If your help centre is a static site whose pages are HTML files in a GitHub repository, Videngine publishes straight into the article. One commit adds the MP4, a poster image and a WebVTT caption track, and writes an HTML5 player, a readable transcript and VideoObject markup into the HTML the server sends. The video SEO guide explains why that matters, and video transcripts and captions covers the text side. Help centres generated by a framework are not supported yet.

Videngine does not publish into hosted help-centre platforms. Download the MP4, with an .srt caption file if the platform's player takes one, and upload it through the platform's editor. Or publish to YouTube as unlisted and embed it: YouTube's privacy settings help says unlisted videos can be seen and shared by anyone with the link and stay out of YouTube search unless added to a public playlist.

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.2.2 asks for captions on prerecorded video at Level A, except where the video is a media alternative for text, presenting nothing the text does not, and is clearly labelled as such. Videngine captions every video from its narration, so caption your support videos and leave the exception alone.

A how-to video maker workflow, from sitemap to published

Eight steps take a knowledge base from its sitemap to published tutorials, and the check against the live product is the one not to skip.

  1. Set up the workspace once. Choose the software and apps workspace, set brand typefaces, colours, logo and motion as tokens, add product names to the lexicon and set a target length.
  2. Read the sitemap. Videngine lists the articles in your help centre's sitemap. Pick a first batch of task articles using the table above.
  3. Refresh the screenshots. Match them to the current interface. Fix the article, not the video.
  4. Draft. Videngine writes a script and timed scene plan from each article. Accept it, edit a line, or paste your own script.
  5. Render. Narration uses a synthetic voice or your own recorded audio, with captions from the finished audio. Output is MP4 or WebM up to 1080p, typically 16:9 for the article and 9:16 for in-app or social use.
  6. Check against the live product. Someone who uses the feature follows the video on the current build; any mismatch goes back into the article.
  7. Publish. Publish into the article on a static site, download for a hosted help centre, deliver to a Google Drive folder you own, or queue to YouTube as unlisted.
  8. Rebuild on change. When an article is edited, re-render it; the new version sits beside the old one in the ledger.

A one-minute 1080p video typically renders in a few minutes, so review sets the pace. The URL-to-video guide covers the same process for a single page.

When screen recording is better

Record the screen when the movement is the lesson: dragging, live typing, a cursor path, or a state that only exists while something loads.

Videngine narrates over screenshots and images; it does not record your screen, follow a cursor or click through a live app. Use a screen recorder when:

  • The step is a gesture, such as dragging a card between columns.
  • Timing is part of the instruction: a progress bar, or a message that disappears.
  • It answers one ticket. Recording a reply is faster than writing an article first.
  • You need a person on camera or another language. Videngine has no presenters or avatars and narrates in English only.

For quick replies, Loom records your screen, webcam or both and shares the result as a link. For polished tutorials, Camtasia records screen, camera and audio as separate layers and adds cursor effects and on-screen annotations. Many teams use both: a recording where the motion matters, rendered videos where a screenshot and a clear sentence do the job.

Questions about how-to videos from help articles

What is the best way to make how-to videos from help articles?

Start with task articles of three to eight steps on a stable screen, one video per article and one step per scene, with narration that names each click by its exact label. Keep the article as the source, so a tutorial video maker can re-render the video when the article changes.

Can a tutorial video maker keep videos up to date when the UI changes?

It can if the video is rendered from the article rather than edited by hand. In Videngine you update the article and its screenshots, then re-render; the rebuild is saved as a new version beside the original. Re-rendering the same video in the same month is free.

Should help center videos replace the written article?

No. Put the video above the written steps and keep the text, because readers scan, search and copy from it, and search engines and AI assistants read it. The video serves people who want to see the screen first.

Can I add knowledge base videos to a hosted help centre?

Yes, but not by publishing from Videngine, which publishes only into static sites whose pages are HTML files in a GitHub repository. Download the MP4 and upload it through your platform's editor, with an .srt caption file if its player accepts one, or publish the video to YouTube as unlisted and embed it.
