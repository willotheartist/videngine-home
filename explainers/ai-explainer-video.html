AI explainer video maker: explainers from what you have written

The short answer

An explainer video maker turns a written explanation into a short narrated video: the problem, how it works, an example and one next step. An AI explainer video maker uses AI for the production work: drafting a spoken script from your text, planning scenes with timings, reading the narration in a synthetic voice and writing captions from the audio. It cannot tell whether the explanation is right; a person who knows the subject still has to check it. Videngine builds motion-graphics explainers this way from a page or text you already have, such as a help article or release note, using your own images rather than generated footage.

What an explainer video maker builds, and where to start

An explainer video makes one thing understood well enough that the viewer can act on it. Whichever explainer video maker you use, most good explainers make the same four moves.

  • Problem: a situation the viewer recognises. “Every Monday, someone exports the same report by hand.”
  • How it works: the steps or the mechanism, in the order the viewer would meet them.
  • Proof or example: one concrete case, a screenshot of the result, or a before and after. Skip it and the viewer has to take the narration on trust.
  • Next step: one action and where to take it. A link to the full article beats “find out more”.

Most teams already have the source. The job is to adapt it, not to invent it.

Explainer typeSource you already haveKeep it to
Product or feature explainerThe feature page or product pageOne feature and its outcome
Help-centre articleThe article itselfOne task, with its screenshots
Release noteThe changelog entry or release postWhat changed and what the viewer now does
Onboarding or training moduleThe written procedure or handbook sectionOne task per video, as a short series
Report or guide summaryThe executive summary or the guideThe main finding and where to read the rest

Length follows the job: stop when the viewer can act, and if an explanation needs longer, split it into a short series. What AI does in an AI explainer video

In an AI explainer video, AI does the drafting and the production, and a person stays responsible for whether the explanation is correct.

A clumsy sentence is obvious on the first watch. A missing condition or an invented number is not, and it reaches every viewer.

JobWhat AI does wellWhat a person still owns
ScriptTurns written text into spoken sentences at a target lengthWhether every sentence is true, and what was cut
Scene planSplits the script into timed scenes and picks a card or image for each lineWhether each picture shows what its line says
VoiceReads the script in a consistent synthetic voiceHow names are said, and whether a human voice suits the subject better
CaptionsTranscribes the finished audio into timed captionsSpelling of names and terms
Title and descriptionDrafts both from the scriptWhether the title promises what the video delivers

The script row is where most of the editing happens. A sentence that reads well on a page often sounds wrong aloud, so the spoken version needs one idea per scene, short sentences with the subject and verb early, and numbers said the way people say them, with the exact figure on a card. A good rewrite is plainer, not looser: the one below still tells the viewer who can do it, where to find it and which version they need.

Illustrative rewrite

Written: “Users with Administrator permissions can now configure SAML-based SSO via the Security tab (v4.2+).”

Spoken: “If you're an admin, you can now set up single sign-on. Open Settings, then Security. You'll need version four point two or later.”

A draft that drops “version four point two or later” sounds just as clear and is wrong for anyone on an older version, which is why the accuracy review below checks for dropped conditions.

Four kinds of explainer video AI, and their risks

Explainer video AI falls into four approaches, which differ less in how the script is written than in where the pictures come from.

ApproachWhat the AI makesGood atRisks
Avatar presenterA synthetic person who reads your script on screenA face and direct address without a shootThe face does not show the product; a realistic person raises disclosure and consent questions
Generated footageNew clips made from a text promptMood and metaphor for abstract ideasCan show interfaces or events that do not exist; results vary between runs
Animated charactersIllustrated people acting out a situationStories, a friendly toneRarely shows the real product; scenes take work to keep up to date
Motion graphics from your textAnimated title cards, information cards and comparisons over your own imagesReal products, processes and documents, at volumeLess emotional range; only as good as your screenshots and images

The deciding question is what the viewer needs to see. If the subject is your product or process, the pictures should be the real thing, not invented footage. Our text-to-video AI guide separates generating footage from narrating a script, and animated explainer videos compares the visual styles.

Labelling depends on the approach too. YouTube's help page on disclosing AI-generated content says non-realistic content made with AI needs no disclosure, and lists production help such as a script, caption creation and idea generation among uses that need no label. Realistic content, such as a scene that did not happen, does. The list is not exhaustive, so check it before publishing a realistic presenter or synthetic voice.

How to brief an AI explainer video maker

Give the AI three things: one question the video answers, the source it must stay within, and the audience.

  • One question per video. A video that answers two questions answers both less well, and is harder to update. If the source covers five tasks, that is five videos.
  • The source. A specific page or block of text, not a topic, so the draft comes from what you wrote. Cut a long source to the part that answers the question; turning an article into a video covers that edit.
  • The audience. Who is watching and what they already know. Without it, a model pitches at a generic reader.
An example brief

Question: What will I pay if I upgrade part-way through a month?

Source: the help article on changing plans, current version only.

Audience: account owners on a monthly plan. They know their plan name, not the word “proration”.

Next step: open the billing page, which shows the exact amount before you confirm.

Leave out: annual plans and refunds, which have their own articles.

The last line matters: without it, a helpful model may add a statement about refunds that is true of some businesses and false of yours. If you would rather write the words yourself, how to write an explainer video script covers writing for the ear, and the AI can voice and caption a script it does not change.

Accuracy review: a checklist before anything goes out

Review an AI explainer against its source, line by line, with the person who owns the subject; reading it for tone is a different check.

Google's guidance on using generative AI content, written for web pages, gives the reason: generative models don't retrieve facts but predict a likely sequence of words, so manual fact-checking before publishing is critical. A narrated video needs the same.

  1. Check the source is current. The AI is only as right as the page it read; if the article is behind the product, fix the article first.
  2. Trace every claim to the source. Cut anything you cannot point to, or have the subject owner confirm it. Plausible extra detail is the hardest error to spot.
  3. Look for dropped conditions. Shortening removes qualifiers such as “admins only” or “within 30 days”, and a true statement becomes false.
  4. Check numbers, names and versions on the cards and in the narration.
  5. Match pictures to words. Screenshots should show today's interface, and each comparison the right thing on each side.
  6. Listen with the sound on for pronunciation, and for stress that changes the meaning.
  7. Watch with the sound off. A viewer on mute gets only the captions and on-screen text.
  8. Check the title and description. They are AI-written too.
  9. Get sign-off from the subject owner, the person who would answer the support ticket.

Record which version of the source each video came from, so you know when a video is out of date.

Keep each explainer in step with its source

The written document stays the source of truth, and the video is a rendering of it. When a feature or procedure changes, change the document first, then re-render the video from it, so the two never drift apart.

  • Versions: Videngine saves a rebuild as a new version, such as _v2, beside the original. Re-rendering the same video in the same month is free. Note which revision of the document each version came from.
  • One task per video: a series of five short explainers is easier to maintain than one long one. If step three of a procedure changes, you re-render the step-three video and leave the rest alone.
  • Captions: W3C's captions guidance describes captions as a text version of the speech and non-speech audio needed to understand the content, and distinguishes open captions, always displayed, from closed captions viewers can turn off. Burned-in captions are open; an .srt sidecar gives closed captions where your player supports it.
  • Voice: a synthetic voice keeps re-renders consistent. A recorded voice means re-recording whenever the source changes.

For tutorials, how-to videos from help articles covers what to do when a label is renamed or a control moves, and training videos from your documents covers procedures and modules.

Using Videngine as an explainer video maker, step by step

As an explainer video maker, Videngine takes a page or text you already have to a reviewed MP4 in seven steps. AI drafts the script and the scene plan; you approve them before anything renders, and review the finished video before it goes out.

  1. Choose the source. One help article or feature page URL, plain text or markdown, or a batch of pages picked from your sitemap.
  2. Read the source. A headless browser keeps the text, images and structured data, and drops navigation and cookie banners.
  3. Approve the script and scene plan. Accept the draft, edit a line at a time, or replace it with your own script. Each brand keeps a pronunciation lexicon for product names, banned phrases, positive-only wording and a target length, so the fiftieth explainer sounds like the first.
  4. Record the narration. The brand's house voice, another synthetic voice or audio you upload. Narration is English only.
  5. Caption from the audio. Captions are transcribed from the finished narration, so they match what was said. Burn them in, export an .srt file, or both.
  6. Render. MP4 (H.264) or WebM at 16:9, 9:16, 1:1 or 4:5, up to 1080p. A one-minute 1080p video typically renders in a few minutes.
  7. Review and deliver. Every video is reviewed in the studio before release, then downloaded, delivered to a Google Drive folder you own, or published to a connected YouTube channel. For YouTube, the script step also writes separate titles, descriptions, hashtags and tags for the Short and the long-form cut.

When Videngine is the wrong tool

Videngine is the wrong tool when the explainer needs characters, a presenter, a hand-drawn style, footage nobody has filmed, a live screen recording or a one-off piece of craft.

  • Characters and stories. Use a character animation tool; our Videngine vs Animaker comparison shows where that kind of editor wins.
  • A presenter on screen. Use an avatar presenter tool; Videngine vs Synthesia sets out the difference.
  • Whiteboard or hand-drawn. A different craft; the whiteboard video maker guide covers when it works.
  • Footage you do not have. Videngine cannot invent a physical process nobody filmed, and if the pictures are evidence, a generator should not either. Film it once.
  • A click-by-click walkthrough of live software. Videngine narrates over screenshots; it does not record a screen. Use a screen recorder with basic editing.
  • An interactive course. Videngine produces video files, not course packages with quizzes and completion tracking. Use an e-learning authoring tool and embed the video.
  • One flagship brand explainer. When it is made once and bespoke illustration is the point, a motion designer or animation studio fits better.

Videngine fits when many explanations are already written down, in a help centre, release notes or procedures, and each video must match its source.

AI explainer video questions

What is an AI explainer video?

An AI explainer video is a short explanatory video in which AI handles some of the production: the script, voice, captions, scene plan or pictures. Someone who knows the subject still has to check that the explanation is right.

Can an explainer video generator write the script from my document?

Yes, provided you give it one question to answer, the source to stay within and the audience. Check every claim against the source before rendering. In Videngine you can accept the drafted script, edit a line, or paste your own script to be used word for word.

How accurate is explainer video AI?

As accurate as its source and its review. Language models predict likely wording rather than look facts up, so a draft can drop a condition or add a plausible detail. Trace each claim to the source and have the subject owner approve the video.

Do I have to disclose that an explainer video was made with AI?

It depends on the platform and on what the AI made. YouTube's help page says AI used for a script or captions needs no disclosure, while realistic synthetic content does. When Videngine publishes to YouTube, synthetic-content disclosure is a setting.

What is the best AI video explainer approach for a software product?

Usually motion graphics over real screenshots, because the viewer needs to see the interface as it really looks. Generated footage cannot show your product, and an avatar presenter adds a face but not the screen. For a live click-by-click walkthrough, use a screen recorder.

How do I create an explainer video from a help article?

Pick one task the article explains and script it for a listener: the problem, the steps, an example of the result and a next step. Use the article's screenshots as the pictures. In Videngine you paste the article's URL, approve or edit the script and scene plan, and review the render before it goes out.

Is an explainer video maker the same as a video presentation maker?

Not quite. A video presentation maker usually starts from slides and narrates them in order. Videngine starts from a page, text or data and writes its own scene plan, so if your source is a slide deck, a presentation tool that exports video fits better.

Can I use an AI explainer video maker for internal training?

Yes, for training that explains a written procedure: narrated, captioned videos built from the document, one task per video, and re-rendered when the document changes. It fits less well when training needs a known person on camera, a live software walkthrough or interactive quizzes.
